# A/B Pricing Strategy Analysis

## Evaluating eCommerce Pricing Strategies Through A/B Testing

An eCommerce pricing experiment designed to evaluate how different pricing strategies affect customer engagement, conversion rate, average order value (AOV), and revenue per visitor (RPV).

This project uses a **500-session simulated eCommerce dataset** to compare three pricing strategies:

* **Control:** Standard price of $79.99
* **Treatment A:** 20% discount — $63.99
* **Treatment B:** 15% premium — $91.99

The objective is to determine whether lowering or increasing the product price produces better business outcomes and whether the observed differences are statistically significant.

---

## Project Overview

Pricing is one of the most important decisions in eCommerce because changing the product price can affect several business metrics simultaneously.

A lower price may increase customer demand and conversion but reduce the value of each order. On the other hand, a higher price may reduce conversion while increasing Average Order Value.

This project uses an **A/B testing framework** to investigate this trade-off.

The analysis focuses on:

* Add-to-cart rate
* Conversion rate
* Average Order Value (AOV)
* Revenue Per Visitor (RPV)
* Statistical significance
* Customer/device/traffic segmentation
* Business recommendations

The final objective is not simply to identify which group has the highest conversion rate, but to determine whether the observed differences are statistically reliable and commercially meaningful.

---

## Business Problem

The business wants to determine the most effective pricing strategy for a mid-market wireless headphone.

The standard list price is:

**$79.99**

Two alternative pricing strategies were tested:

| Pricing Arm |  Price | Strategy         |
| ----------- | -----: | ---------------- |
| Control     | $79.99 | Standard pricing |
| Treatment A | $63.99 | 20% discount     |
| Treatment B | $91.99 | 15% premium      |

The key business questions are:

1. Does discount pricing increase conversion?
2. Does premium pricing decrease conversion?
3. Does premium pricing increase Average Order Value?
4. Which pricing strategy generates the highest Revenue Per Visitor?
5. Are the differences statistically significant?
6. Should the company change its current pricing strategy?

## Experimental Design

The experiment follows a **three-arm A/B testing structure**.

Visitors were assigned to one of three pricing groups.

| Group       |  Price | Visitors | Purpose               |
| ----------- | -----: | -------: | --------------------- |
| Control     | $79.99 |      175 | Baseline              |
| Treatment A | $63.99 |      165 | Test discount pricing |
| Treatment B | $91.99 |      160 | Test premium pricing  |
| **Total**   |      — |  **500** | —                     |

The price was assigned at the visitor-session level.

The experiment was designed to prevent a visitor from seeing multiple prices during the same session.

# Hypotheses

## Hypothesis 1 — Discount vs Control

**Null Hypothesis (H0):**

Discount pricing produces the same conversion rate as standard pricing.

**Alternative Hypothesis (H1):**

Discount pricing increases conversion rate.

---

## Hypothesis 2 — Premium vs Control

**Null Hypothesis (H0):**

Premium pricing produces the same conversion rate as standard pricing.

**Alternative Hypothesis (H1):**

Premium pricing decreases conversion rate but increases Average Order Value.

---

## Hypothesis 3 — Revenue Per Visitor

**Null Hypothesis (H0):**

Revenue Per Visitor is equal across all three pricing groups.

**Alternative Hypothesis (H1):**

Revenue Per Visitor differs between the pricing groups.

---

# Analysis Methodology

The project uses several statistical techniques to evaluate the experiment.

### 1. Conversion Rate

Conversion Rate measures the percentage of visitors who completed a purchase.

**Formula:**

`Conversion Rate = Conversions / Total Visitors × 100`

---

### 2. Add-to-Cart Rate

Measures the percentage of visitors who added the product to their cart.

**Formula:**

`Add-to-Cart Rate = Add-to-Cart Users / Total Visitors × 100`

---

### 3. Average Order Value

AOV measures the average revenue generated per completed order.

**Formula:**

`AOV = Total Order Revenue / Number of Orders`

---

### 4. Revenue Per Visitor

RPV measures revenue generated per visitor, including visitors who did not purchase.

**Formula:**

`RPV = Total Revenue / Total Visitors`

RPV is particularly useful for pricing experiments because it combines the effects of conversion and order value.

---

## Statistical Tests

The following statistical methods were used:

### Two-Proportion Z-Test

Used to compare conversion rates between two pricing groups.

Examples:

* Control vs Discount
* Control vs Premium
* Discount vs Premium

---

### Chi-Square Test

Used to determine whether conversion is associated with pricing group across all three groups simultaneously.

---

### One-Way ANOVA

Used to determine whether Average Order Value differs significantly between the three pricing groups.

---

### Welch's T-Test

Used for the pairwise comparison of AOV between Control and Premium because the groups have unequal variance.

---

### Wilson Score Confidence Interval

95% Wilson confidence intervals were calculated for conversion rates to provide a more reliable estimate of conversion uncertainty.

# Results

## Funnel and Conversion Analysis

The experiment produced the following results:

| Metric           |   Control |   Discount |   Premium |
| ---------------- | --------: | ---------: | --------: |
| Visitors         |       175 |        165 |       160 |
| Add-to-Cart Rate |     28.6% |  **38.8%** |     19.4% |
| Conversions      |        25 |         25 |        16 |
| Conversion Rate  |     14.3% |  **15.2%** |     10.0% |
| 95% CI           | 9.9–20.2% | 10.5–21.4% | 6.2–15.6% |

### Key Observation

The Discount group achieved:

* Highest Add-to-Cart Rate: **38.8%**
* Highest raw Conversion Rate: **15.2%**

The Premium group had:

* Lowest Add-to-Cart Rate: **19.4%**
* Lowest Conversion Rate: **10.0%**

However, the higher observed conversion rate for Discount pricing does **not automatically mean that discount pricing is statistically better**.

---

# Statistical Significance Results

| Statistical Test                     |  Statistic | p-value | Significant? |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------: | ------: | ------------ |
| Chi-Square: Conversion vs Group      | χ² = 2.158 |   0.340 | No           |
| Z-Test: Control vs Discount          | z = -0.225 |   0.822 | No           |
| Z-Test: Control vs Premium           |  z = 1.196 |   0.232 | No           |
| Z-Test: Discount vs Premium          |  z = 1.398 |   0.162 | No           |
| ANOVA: AOV vs Group                  | F = 14.279 | <0.0001 | **Yes**      |
| Welch T-Test: Control vs Premium AOV | t = -2.922 |   0.006 | **Yes**      |

The significance level used was:

**α = 0.05**

---

# Interpretation

## Conversion Rate

Although the Discount group had a slightly higher conversion rate than Control:

**Discount: 15.2%**

vs.

**Control: 14.3%**

the difference was not statistically significant.

The Control vs Discount z-test produced:

**p = 0.822**

Since:

`0.822 > 0.05`

we fail to reject the null hypothesis.

Therefore, the dataset does **not provide sufficient evidence that the discount strategy genuinely increases conversion**.

---

## Premium Pricing

Premium pricing produced a lower conversion rate:

**Premium: 10.0%**

vs.

**Control: 14.3%**

However, the difference was also not statistically significant.

Control vs Premium:

**p = 0.232**

Therefore, the experiment cannot conclusively prove that premium pricing reduces conversion.

---

# Average Order Value Analysis

A different pattern appears when analyzing AOV.

| Pricing Group | Average Order Value |
| ------------- | ------------------: |
| Discount      |              $77.32 |
| Control       |              $90.52 |
| Premium       |         **$101.29** |

Premium pricing generated the highest Average Order Value.

The ANOVA test produced:

**F = 14.279**

with:

**p < 0.0001**

This indicates a statistically significant difference in AOV across the pricing groups.

The Control vs Premium Welch's t-test also confirmed the difference:

**t = -2.922**

**p = 0.006**

Therefore, premium pricing produced a statistically significant increase in order value compared with the Control group.

---

# Revenue Per Visitor

Revenue Per Visitor was calculated using:

`RPV = Total Revenue / Total Visitors`

Results:

| Pricing Group | Revenue Per Visitor |
| ------------- | ------------------: |
| Control       |          **$12.93** |
| Discount      |              $11.72 |
| Premium       |              $10.13 |

Based on this simulated dataset, **Control pricing generated the highest RPV**.

### RPV Ranking

1. Control — **$12.93**
2. Discount — **$11.72**
3. Premium — **$10.13**

However, the RPV differences should be interpreted carefully because the underlying conversion-rate differences were not statistically significant.

Additionally, RPV does not include product cost or profit margin.

# Sample Size Limitation

One of the most important findings from the experiment is that the test is relatively small.

The experiment contains only:

**500 total sessions**

or approximately:

* 175 Control
* 165 Discount
* 160 Premium

For a live experiment, a larger sample would be required to confidently detect relatively small conversion-rate changes.

The report estimates that approximately:

**900–1,000 sessions per arm**

would be needed to detect a 3-percentage-point conversion lift with approximately 80% power at α = 0.05.

Therefore, the current results should be treated as **directional rather than conclusive**.

---

# Business Recommendation

Based on the current experiment:

### Short-Term Recommendation

Do not declare a conversion-rate winner.

The conversion differences are not statistically significant.

Instead, continue the experiment with a larger sample.

---

### Pricing Recommendation

A modest premium/value-based strategy can be considered while additional testing is conducted because premium pricing generated significantly higher AOV.

However, the current data does not prove that premium pricing is the best overall strategy because its RPV was lower than Control.

---

### Future Experiment

The next experiment should:

* Increase sample size
* Continue monitoring conversion rate
* Analyze device-level performance
* Analyze traffic-source performance
* Monitor session engagement
* Include product COGS
* Calculate contribution margin per visitor
* Test different premium/discount levels
* Consider dynamic pricing for high-intent customers

---

# Profitability Consideration

Revenue alone should not be the final pricing decision metric.

The current analysis uses:

**Revenue Per Visitor**

but does not include product cost.

A more useful metric for the next experiment would be:

`Contribution Margin Per Visitor = (Revenue - COGS) / Visitors`

This would allow the business to compare the profitability of discount versus premium pricing.

# What I Learned

This project helped demonstrate that a Data Analyst should not rely only on the highest percentage or largest number.

The Discount group had the highest raw conversion rate, but statistical testing showed that the difference was not significant.

At the same time, Premium pricing produced a significantly higher Average Order Value.

This highlights an important principle of A/B testing:

**A higher metric does not necessarily mean a better strategy. Statistical significance, sample size, business context, and profitability must also be considered.**

# Conclusion

This A/B pricing experiment demonstrates the trade-off between **conversion and order value**.

The Discount strategy produced the highest add-to-cart and raw conversion rates, but the conversion advantage was not statistically significant.

Premium pricing produced the highest Average Order Value and showed statistically significant AOV differences.

However, the **Control price generated the highest Revenue Per Visitor** in the current simulated experiment.

Therefore, the best decision is not to immediately change the pricing strategy. The experiment should be extended with a larger sample and additional profitability metrics before making a permanent pricing decision.

The ultimate goal should be to identify the pricing strategy that maximizes **contribution margin per visitor**, rather than simply maximizing conversion rate or order value.
